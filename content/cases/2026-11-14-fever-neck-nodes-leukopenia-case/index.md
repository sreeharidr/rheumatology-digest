## 1. The Presentation

A 19-year-old woman has had **three weeks** of painful swelling on both sides of her neck, worse on the left, with pain referred to the left ear. In primary care she is afebrile with normal vital signs. Both posterior cervical chains hold tender, mobile nodes, the largest about 2 × 1 cm on the left. Ears, throat and tonsils look normal. Rapid streptococcal, influenza and SARS-CoV-2 tests are negative. **White-cell count 2900/µL** (4000–11,000); haemoglobin and platelets are normal. EBV serology shows past infection, and CMV is nonreactive.

## 2. Ultrasound and a First Treatment

**Neck ultrasound** shows mildly enlarged oval nodes with preserved fatty hila and increased vascularity. In the left lower jugular chain (level IV) they are clustered, with indistinct margins and swollen fat around them. There is **no drainable collection**. She is given amoxicillin–clavulanate, ibuprofen and paracetamol, but the pain persists. Four days before admission she develops fever up to 39.5°C, drenching night sweats and exhausting fatigue, and attends the paediatric emergency department.

## 3. The Emergency Department

Temperature 38.3°C, pulse 71 a minute, other vital signs normal. She has **shaking chills** under several blankets. The neck nodes are tender and neck movement is normal. White-cell count 2710/µL, **haemoglobin 11.5 g/dL** (12.0–16.0), platelets normal. The chest radiograph shows no lung disease and no hilar or mediastinal widening. Blood cultures are drawn, vancomycin, ampicillin–sulbactam and fluids are started, and she is admitted to paediatrics.

## 4. The Fuller History

She reports persistent fever and fatigue, **aching in both wrists and proximal interphalangeal joints** without swelling, and **tenderness over the left Achilles tendon**. She has no cough, rash, mouth or genital ulcers, dry eyes or mouth, Raynaud phenomenon, hair loss, diarrhoea or abdominal pain. She has anxiety, treated with sertraline. She was raised in Canada and now lives in Massachusetts with her mother and **two dogs**. She has not travelled outside New England except to Canada, has no tuberculosis contacts, is not sexually active and uses no alcohol, tobacco or drugs. Her mother has Graves disease, and a cousin has juvenile rheumatoid arthritis.

## 5. The Examination

Oral temperature 38.4°C, blood pressure 110/56, pulse 95, respiratory rate 20, oxygen saturation 100% on air; weight 48.3 kg (BMI 20.5). She looks tired but is not in distress. The throat is clear with no mouth ulcers and the neck is supple. Nodes are palpable on **both sides** in the cervical chains, with **new submandibular and supraclavicular nodes**, none fluctuant. There are no axillary, epitrochlear or inguinal nodes. Heart, lungs and abdomen are unremarkable. The **wrists, PIP joints and left Achilles tendon are tender without synovitis.** No rash.

## 6. First Laboratory Results

**CRP 25.5 mg/L** (under 8) and **ESR 38 mm/h** (0–19). Neutrophils 1450/µL, lymphocytes 1030/µL, haemoglobin 11.5 g/dL, platelets 221,000/µL. **AST 62 and ALT 57 U/L** are both raised, and **LDH is 732 U/L** (110–210). Creatinine, uric acid, C3 and C4 are normal, and so is the **ferritin (172 µg/L)**. Urinalysis is bland. ANA, ANCA, anti-CCP and rheumatoid factor are negative. Blood cultures show no growth; HIV antigen/antibody, syphilis, hepatitis B and C, and an interferon-γ release assay are negative.

While tissue is arranged, the team works through the differential. **Infection** comes first: bacterial lymphadenitis, cat scratch disease (*Bartonella*), gonococcal pharyngitis, EBV, CMV, acute HIV, and tuberculosis or non-tuberculous mycobacteria. Cultures, serologies and the antibiotic trial have each been unrewarding.

## 9. The Differential: Cancer

**Malignancy** stays on the list because of fever, persistent nodes and a high LDH. Hodgkin lymphoma peaks between ages 15 and 29. Non-Hodgkin lymphomas and acute leukaemias are less common or less likely in this age group and with this blood picture.

## 10. The Differential: Rheumatic Disease

**Inflammatory conditions** are next. Kawasaki disease is a disease of young children. Felty syndrome needs long-standing seropositive rheumatoid arthritis and splenomegaly. Sarcoidosis nearly always shows chest imaging abnormalities. Still's disease is unlikely with a normal ferritin, and reactive arthritis has no preceding infection. **Systemic lupus** matches the picture best: fever, nodes, arthralgia, leukopenia and raised transaminases.

## 11. The Leading Diagnosis

Fever, cervical lymphadenopathy and leukopenia in a young adult is the classic triad of **Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease**, also called histiocytic necrotizing lymphadenitis. It is rare, usually self-limited and of unknown cause, thought to follow an exaggerated immune response to a viral trigger such as human herpesvirus 6 or 8, EBV or parvovirus B19. The nodes may or may not be painful, are usually unilateral, and can look necrotic on imaging, which can be mistaken for cancer.

{{< case-mcq >}}
question: |
  How is Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease confirmed?
options:
  - text: A positive ANA and anti-dsDNA
    rationale: |
      These point to lupus, which is a mimic, and a negative ANA here argues against it. They do not confirm this disease.
  - text: Serology for the possible viral triggers
    rationale: |
      Several viruses have been proposed as triggers, but no virological test confirms the diagnosis. Testing mainly serves to exclude active infection.
  - text: Imaging that shows nodal necrosis
    rationale: |
      Necrosis on imaging is the very feature that can be mistaken for cancer. It raises suspicion without proving anything.
  - text: A lymph-node biopsy, with excision preferred over fine-needle aspiration
    correct: true
    rationale: |
      The diagnosis is **histological**. Ultrasound-guided aspiration is sometimes enough, but excisional biopsy is generally preferred because it preserves nodal architecture, shows the histology, and lets lymphoproliferative disease be ruled out in the same specimen.
{{< /case-mcq >}}

## 12. The Biopsy

Fine-needle aspiration and core biopsy of a left neck node yield little: the core shows only clot and the aspirate is paucicellular. A few macrophages are odd, with **crescent-shaped, sharply angulated nuclei** pushed aside by nuclear debris, unlike ordinary tingible-body macrophages. A cell block shows a focus of necrosis. Because the material is limited, an **excisional biopsy** is done a few days later.

{{< case-mcq >}}
question: |
  Which histological pattern in the excised node best fits the diagnosis?
options:
  - text: Suppurative necrosis with neutrophils
    rationale: |
      That points to infections such as plague, tularaemia, cat scratch disease, lymphogranuloma venereum and some mycobacterial and fungal infections. The absence of neutrophils is one of the features that separates this disease from them.
  - text: Non-caseating granulomas
    rationale: |
      Granulomas suggest sarcoidosis, certain infections, a foreign-body reaction or some tumours. None were seen.
  - text: Patchy paracortical necrosis with abundant karyorrhectic debris, crescentic histiocytes and no neutrophils or granulomas
    correct: true
    rationale: |
      This is the histological signature of **Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease**. Stains for CMV, EBV and HSV, acid-fast and silver stains, cultures, flow cytometry and immunohistochemistry were all negative, so infection and lymphoproliferative disease were excluded as well.
  - text: Sheets of atypical lymphoid cells on flow cytometry
    rationale: |
      That would indicate a lymphoproliferative process. Flow cytometry and immunostaining in this case showed none.

## 13. Treatment

Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease is most often benign and self-limited, lasting **one to four months**. She still has fever, fatigue and joint pain, and treatment should control symptoms without compromising investigation of other diseases not yet fully excluded.

## 14. Looking Ahead

After the biopsy she takes **naproxen for two weeks**. Three days after discharge her fever has gone, and it does not return. The nodes do not come back.

## 15. Outcome and Take-Aways

**Outcome.** At five months she was well and her repeat ANA was negative. A rheumatology visit was booked for a year later (17 months after symptom onset), with autoantibody monitoring for early signs of SLE.

**Take-aways**

- **Fever, tender cervical nodes and leukopenia** in a young adult is the classic triad for Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease, but it must be diagnosed after infection and malignancy are excluded.
- **Failure of antibiotics, necrotic-looking nodes and an unrevealing non-invasive workup** mean the next step is tissue, and an excisional biopsy is preferred over aspiration.
- **A normal ferritin** argues against Still's disease and macrophage activation syndrome, and **a negative ANA in a systemically ill patient** argues against lupus.
- **The histology is characteristic:** paracortical necrosis with karyorrhectic debris, crescentic histiocytes, and no neutrophils or granulomas.
- **Treatment is symptomatic:** NSAIDs first, with glucocorticoids reserved for severe or uncontrolled disease once infection and cancer are excluded.
- **It can recur, and it can precede SLE.** About 3% of patients have SLE, and in about a third of those the lupus appears after the nodal illness, so rheumatology follow-up with autoantibody checks is worthwhile.
